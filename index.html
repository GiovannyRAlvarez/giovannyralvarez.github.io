About

Welcome! I study why violence persists and changes shape rather than ending when civil wars do. I am currently a Visiting Assistant Professor at the University of St. Thomas in Houston, and I completed my Ph.D. in Public Policy at the University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill in 2026.

My dissertation, The Price of Elite Peace, develops a dual-arena theory of post-conflict violence. I argue that peace interventions designed to solve elite-level commitment problems, negotiated settlements, and consent-based policies systematically displace risk onto the communities where wars were actually fought. Articles from this project appear in Studies in Conflict & Terrorism and, with coauthors, in the Journal of Conflict Resolution. My work has been recognized with a Dissertation Completion Fellowship from the International Studies Association and a Saperstein Fellowship from Wayne State University.

I am a co-author of the U.S. Aid to Security Sector Actors (USASSA) dataset, which converts unclassified U.S. security assistance records from 2000 onward into analytical typologies that make it possible to evaluate how different forms of security aid affect conflict and human rights outcomes.

I bring an unusual vantage point to these questions. Before graduate school I spent more than fifteen years implementing the policies I now evaluate — coordinating violence prevention for a USAID/Chemonics program in Colombia, working on transitional justice at UNDP, and conducting human rights research at Colombia's Defensoría del Pueblo. I have taught at the undergraduate and graduate levels in both the United States and Colombia, and I teach in English and Spanish.

Fieldwork in Tumaco and the Pacific coast of Colombia, home to Afro-Colombian and indigenous communities disproportionately affected by post-conflict violence. My research examines how peace processes and transitional justice mechanisms affect marginalized populations in remote areas like these.

Selected Research & Manuscripts

Current Research Portfolio

My active research portfolio currently consists of two primary manuscripts developing and testing the Dual-Arena Theory of Post-Conflict Violence. This work explores the divergent effects of transitional justice on political versus societal domains, explaining how amnesties and peace frameworks can inadvertently redistribute violence rather than eliminate it.
