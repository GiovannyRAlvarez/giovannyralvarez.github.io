Teaching

What theory is for

I want students who can take a theory into a real case, find where it breaks, and say what should be done instead.

I spent seventeen years working on peace and conflict before I began teaching full time, including three peace negotiations and the victims and transitional justice chapter of Colombia's 2016 accord with the FARC. I watched carefully designed policies produce outcomes nobody in the room intended. That experience gave me the two objectives below.

Purpose

Two objectives

Every syllabus I write serves these two ends. Everything else is method.

01

Critical thinking

Crossing theory with real cases

I introduce a concept, then hand students a case that strains it. In International Politics we read the standard bargaining account of why wars end, then work through the Colombian negotiation, including the parts that went badly. Students take theory more seriously, not less, when they see a practitioner treat it as something that can fail.

In practice: sequential questioning that starts where every student can answer and ends somewhere they did not expect. Name the trade-off, argue a position, and state the strongest version of the view you reject before attacking it.

02

Policy orientation

Evaluating policy that is currently in force

I do not teach policy as history. Students work with live material: peace agreement texts, Security Council resolutions, congressional budget justifications, national development plans, and program evaluations. These are the documents I used as a practitioner, and most undergraduates have never been asked to read one closely.

In practice: students take an ongoing policy through the four steps below and finish with a memo they can show someone. Not a grade, a writing sample.

How a policy assignment works

Four steps, one live policy

Students choose something currently in force: security assistance to a partner government, an active peace process, a sanctions regime.

Reconstruct

Establish what the policy is actually designed to do, from the primary documents rather than from press coverage.

Surface assumptions

Identify the causal claims the policy depends on to work. Most are never stated outright.

Evaluate

Test whether those assumptions are holding, using the evidence that exists rather than the evidence one would want.

Propose

Argue for a specific alternative, with its costs and its losers stated honestly.

In the classroom

Three commitments

What the two objectives require of me.

Evidence and methods

Both objectives depend on students handling evidence, so I teach causal inference in R and Stata with real data rather than cleaned extracts. My own USASSA dataset is built for undergraduate research, and several of its questions are still open.

AI as a tool, not an authority

Permitted as a research and search tool: exploring a topic, locating sources, deepening understanding of unfamiliar material, always verified against the originals. Not permitted to generate text, analyses, or arguments for submission. One assignment each term has students check AI output against the primary documents and record where it went wrong.

Mentorship

I taught working students and serving police officers in Colombia for seven years before graduate school, and I was an international student myself. I mentor through the American Political Science Association and treat advising as part of the job, not an addition to it.
